Artifact 1: Mathematics Minor

I have enjoyed taking math classes since I started school, so working towards a mathematics minor was a natural progression for me. As an engineering student, I was required to take up through MA 341, so only four extra courses were required.

These are the courses I took for the minor (in addition to the prerequisite calculus classes). The last three courses are of particular note, as they have simultaneously prepared me for graduate-level study, while throwing me into numerous research projects.

  • MA 341 Differential Equations 1 - A course on ordinary differential equations and common solution methods / applications
  • MA 401 Differential Equations 2 - A course on partial differential equations, notably the Wave, Heat, and Laplace equations
  • MA 405 Advanced Linear Algebra - A proof-based study of linear algebra, emphasizing vector spaces and linear maps
  • MA 326 Mathematical Foundations of Data Science 1 - An exploration of the math underpinning several modern data science algorithms, focusing on problems with explicit necessary conditions
  • MA 404 Mathematical Foundations of Data Science 2 - An exploration of optimization algorithms, neural network architectures, generative learning, and transformers (the building blocks behind ChatGPT!)

Artifact 2: MA 326 Adopt an Algorithm Paper (Decision Trees)

MA 326 is a very research-focused course, culminating in an Adopt an Algorithm project. I researched and coded a decision tree algorithm, and synthesized the results in a paper (shown right, click to open in new tab). This was a vital milestone for me, as it was my first time writing a complex research paper on a topic like this.

Decision trees are a tool which systematically and recursively split a dataset, forming a tree structure (as shown left). When new data is introduced, we can compare new points to the tree's split boundaries, tracing a path down the tree. The result at the final node can be used to make meaningful predictions (such as assigning a class) about the input data. If you are interested in a deeper dive, feel free to check out the paper in its entirety (linked right).

Artifact 3: MA 404 Adopt an Algorithm Pesentation (Differential Evolution)

Similarly to its predecessor MA 326, MA 404 builds up to the Adopt an Algorithm at the end of the semester. Since I took this class the first semester it was offered, we had a small class (7, including the instructor), and thus opted to turn the project into a presentation instead of a paper.

To fit the theme of the class, I presented on the differential evolution (DE) algorithm. DE is a metaheuristic used to find a near-optimal solution to a problem, harnessing an evolving "population" of points. You can learn more about this algorithm from my presentation (linked, top left).

As part of my presentation, I made a Colab notebook (linked, top right). The notebook serves to demonstrate DE in action, while providing informative visualizations. Here, you can see DE minimizing a quadratic (bottom left), along with the Ackley function (bottom right), each in two dimensions.

Artifact 4: RISE REU Research + Poster

Continuing the ripple effect of my mathematics minor, the experience from my MA 326 class landed me a summer research internship via the Research in ISE: Research Experience for Undergraduates (RISE REU) program.

On this project, I was tasked with continuing research on combining the strengths of agent-based and compartmental (differential-equation-based) models. The goal of this project is to use the Biologically-Influenced Neural Networks (BINNs) technique to create a hybrid model which captures the behavior of the disease spread simulator Covasim.

To accomplish this goal, I needed to expand the differential equation model of the system to include more biological factors and increase the fidelity of the model. The diagram on the left shows the model used to describe how people transition from healthy to infected to recovered, after the relevant updates were made. I ended up making a research poster on my process and findings, for NCSU's Summer Research Symposium. If you are interested in exploring this work further, you can explore this poster (linked, right).
